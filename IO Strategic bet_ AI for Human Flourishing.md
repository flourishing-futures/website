**\# \*\*AI for Human Flourishing\*\***

**\*\*IO Strategic Bet — Proposal for Innovation Panel\*\***

*\*GovTech Singapore — Innovation Office, June 2026\**

**\---**

**\#\# \*\*The Problem\*\***

AI has genuine potential to help people flourish — to extend what individuals can learn, decide, create, and achieve. A public officer with a well-designed AI tool could reason through more complex problems, serve citizens more thoughtfully, and spend less time on the work that dulls rather than develops. A student with the right AI support could build deeper understanding, not just faster answers. A citizen navigating a major life decision could be genuinely helped to think better, not just handed a recommendation.

That is the prize. The question is whether we are designing toward it.

Singapore has always been deliberate about the qualities it builds in its people. National Service develops resilience and collective responsibility. Meritocracy builds a culture of effort and ownership. These were not accidental outcomes — they were the result of sustained, intentional policy design. The same intentionality must now extend to how we design and deploy AI — because without it, the technology will shape people in ways we did not choose.

The risks operate at multiple levels. At the individual level, systems optimised for ease and task completion can gradually substitute for the struggles that build capability, judgement, and grit. At the organisational level, AI rolled out without deliberate intent can quietly reshape institutional culture, team dynamics, and the pipeline of capability that the public service depends on. At the societal level, algorithmic bias in hiring, healthcare, and housing — documented extensively overseas — poses particular risks in Singapore, where public sector AI touches domains central to fairness, equality, and racial harmony.

All of these risks share the same root: AI deployed without explicit intent about what kind of people, organisations, and society we are building.

The scale makes this urgent. According to the NAIS 2.0 update, PAIR — the government's secure AI assistant — has already reached over 80% of public officers, with 73,000 officers actively using it each month. Over 31,000 custom AI bots have been built across agencies to date. This is not a pilot. AI is now embedded in the daily work of the Singapore public service at a scale that few countries have matched.

These concerns are already being felt. Internal forums and senior leaders in government have raised questions about what sustained AI use is doing to professional judgement — whether the cognitive process of internalising, interpreting, and reasoning through difficult decisions is being gradually hollowed out. Traditional paths for building judgement — entry-level work, note-taking, observing seniors, sitting with ambiguity — may be quietly disappearing. But these concerns remain unstructured. There is no shared framework for thinking about them, no methodology for assessing them, and no systematic plan to address them.

Our current responsible AI frameworks are necessary but insufficient. They focus primarily on preventing direct harm — bias, safety, accuracy, privacy. That floor must be maintained. But there is a ceiling no one is building toward: AI that does not merely avoid harm but actively strengthens the people who use it. The gap between those two things is where the most important work lies.

**\---**

**\#\# \*\*What AI for Human Flourishing Encompasses\*\***

Think of this as an iceberg. The most visible layer sits above the waterline — easy to point to, already being addressed. Below it are deeper layers that are harder to see, less well understood, and carry the highest long-term stakes.

**\---**

**\#\#\# \*\*Level 1 — Responsible AI\*\***

*\*Is this AI safe and fair to deploy?\**

This is the visible tip: the known harms we can name, test for, and guard against. Does the AI produce dangerous outputs — content that facilitates abuse, self-harm, or violence? Does it encode bias that disadvantages certain groups in consequential decisions? Does it handle personal data responsibly? Can it be manipulated through adversarial prompts or jailbreaks?

These are important questions with increasingly established approaches. GovTech's Responsible AI team is already doing serious work at this layer: building and testing guardrails that filter harmful inputs and outputs, developing the RAI Benchmark to evaluate AI systems against safety, fairness, and robustness standards, and producing LionGuard — a content moderation guardrail localised to the Singapore context, including the specific linguistic and cultural nuances that generic international tools miss. Their testing methodology spans safety, fairness, robustness, and agentic behaviour, with a clear ambition to move government AI evaluation from ad hoc checks toward continuous, automated monitoring.

This work is necessary and must be sustained. But it addresses AI as a deployment risk — whether a system is safe enough to release. It does not address what happens to people after it is released, and over time.

*\*How it is addressed: directly controlled and managed — through security practices, risk frameworks, and governance policy.\**

**\---**

**\#\#\# \*\*Level 2 — Human-AI Collaboration\*\***

*\*Does this interaction make people more or less capable?\**

This layer sits at the waterline: partially recognised, but significantly under-designed for. It concerns what happens to a person *\*in the moment of interacting\** with AI — the immediate cognitive, psychological, and behavioural effects of how AI tools are built and how they behave.

Traditional human-computer interaction (HCI) design has decades of practice in making software usable: reducing friction, lowering cognitive load, designing for clarity. But AI introduces dynamics that existing HCI practice was not built for. When an AI produces a lengthy, confident output, does it help the person think — or does it create a new bottleneck, where human review becomes overwhelming and people defer rather than engage? When an AI drafts a recommendation, does the design of the interaction invite scrutiny and judgement, or does it subtly encourage acceptance?

The questions here include: how should AI tools be designed to support rather than replace human reasoning? How do we lower cognitive load without removing the productive struggle that builds capability? What interaction patterns build appropriate trust — neither blind reliance nor reflexive rejection? How do we help people stay meaningfully in the loop? When a design tool produces one polished answer to a brief, does it help the designer explore — or does it quietly close down the divergent thinking that good creative work depends on?

These are not purely engineering questions. They sit at the intersection of design, psychology, and policy — and they are largely unaddressed in Singapore's current AI deployment.

*\*How it is addressed: designed intentionally — through product design, interaction design, and HCI practice that is built for AI, not borrowed from earlier software paradigms.\**

**\---**

**\#\#\# \*\*Level 3 — Organisational / Institutional Impact\*\***

*\*What kind of public service is AI shaping?\**

This layer is less visible and doesn't happen immediately, but it can emerge quickly as AI rolls out across an organisation.

When AI is deployed at scale, it does not just change how individuals work. It gradually reshapes team dynamics, professional norms, and organisational culture. As AI takes on more of the analytical and drafting work, the nature of collaboration changes: if officers are increasingly working alongside AI rather than with each other, what happens to the team-based reasoning and collective judgement that good policy and service design depend on? If junior officers spend less time on the foundational work that builds understanding — and more time reviewing AI outputs — what does the pipeline of experienced public servants look like in ten years? And as some officers embrace AI to move faster while others resist out of concern for craft or anxiety about relevance, what happens to the cohesion and shared culture of the organisation?

The public sector has a particular responsibility here. Unlike private companies, which have been willing to dramatically reduce headcount and restructure around AI efficiency, the public service cannot and should not optimise purely for output. Its legitimacy depends on human judgement, accountability, and the trust of citizens. The question at this level is not just how to manage the change, but what kind of institution Singapore's public service should become as AI embeds itself into how it works — and whether we are making that choice deliberately.

*\*How it is addressed: influenced and shaped — through Human-AI team building, organisation design, decision making structures, incentives & performance management, leadership intent, and deliberate culture-building.\**

**\---**

**\#\#\# \*\*Level 4 — Societal Impact\*\***

*\*What kind of people and culture will emerge in the long-term?\**

The deepest layer is the hardest to see — and carries the longest shadow. This is about cumulative effects that play out over years: what sustained AI use does to human agency, creativity, wellbeing, social relationships, and cultural norms.

Some signals are already emerging. Studies show that people tend to accept AI recommendations with less scrutiny than equivalent human advice, even when the AI is wrong. Reliance on AI for writing and creative work may be producing a convergence toward the average — a flattening of voice and originality. The worry about AI companions substituting for human relationships is no longer hypothetical; it is already being designed for, including in Singapore.

These effects are difficult to measure, easy to deprioritise in the urgency to scale, and potentially very hard to reverse once entrenched. Understanding them requires deliberate, Singapore-contextualised research — not as an academic exercise, but with clear learning and feedback loops to policy, practice, development, deployment.

*\*How it is addressed: understood and responded to — through sustained research, carefully designed experiments, and policy that is willing to act on what the evidence shows.\**

**\---**

**\*\*The gap\*\***

**\*\*No single team can own all four levels today.\*\***

The Responsible AI team (@AI Practice) and AI policy (@NAIG) leads on Level 1\.   
AI design, development and deployment teams (@GovTech/Agencies) are best placed to advance Level 2\.   
Senior leadership, transformation, people capability, organisation development are shaping Level 3 outcomes (@Agencies and WOG).   
Level 4 requires systemic and co-ordinated actions across the public and private sector (WOG and beyond).

What is needed is a programme that holds the whole picture — ensuring these layers are addressed in concert rather than in isolation, and that progress at each level informs the others.

The Innovation Office is willing to serve as the initiator, helping to orchestrate the different internal teams at GovTech, relevant parties across the Government, and external research partners, to catalyse action and drive the initial progress — with the goal to transition ownership to the right long-term owner when ready.

**\---**

**\#\# \*\*What This Means for Singapore\*\***

The four levels above describe the problem space. But the more uncomfortable question is: what does Singapore actually believe about what AI should do to people — and have we said so?

Most AI deployment in Singapore today is driven by clear hypotheses at the efficiency layer: AI will help officers work faster, reduce backlogs, and improve service quality. Those hypotheses are reasonable and worth pursuing. But at the human development layer — what AI does to people's capabilities, relationships, and sense of self over time — we have no equivalent clarity. We are scaling without a position.

A few domains illustrate immediately where this matters.

**\*\*Public officers and judgement quality.\*\*** The working hypothesis behind tools like PAIR is that AI will improve productivity and the quality of public service. That may well be true. But we have not stated what we are willing to protect in the process — specifically, the quality of professional judgement that makes officers effective in genuinely difficult situations. Judgement is built through lived experience: sitting with ambiguous decisions, making calls without complete information, learning from being wrong. If AI handles those moments instead, the productivity gains may be real while the capability loss is invisible — until it matters.

**\*\*Education and the development of capability.\*\*** The tension here is real and not yet fully resolved. Students who use AI tools may produce faster, better-polished work — and risk being left behind if they don't engage with these tools at all. But the struggle that AI can remove — forming an argument, sitting with a hard problem, revising work repeatedly — may be precisely what builds the cognitive capability we want students to develop. Some countries have responded by pulling back from digital tools in classrooms entirely. The question of what role AI should play in Singapore's educational model, and what it should be designed to protect, deserves an explicit answer.

**\*\*Social cohesion and human connection.\*\*** AI companions for lonely or elderly residents are already being proposed and built — including in recent GovTech hackathon projects. The intention is genuinely good: reducing isolation, providing support, improving quality of life for people who live alone. But the long-term question is unasked: if AI companions substitute for human relationships rather than supplementing them, what does that do to social participation, community bonds, and the fabric of mutual obligation that holds a society together? We do not know. And we are building before we do.

The point is not that these are reasons to slow down. It is that Singapore has always been deliberate about the qualities it builds in its people — and that same deliberateness must now extend to these questions. Deploying AI at scale without explicit hypotheses about human development is not neutral. It is a choice, made by default.

**\#\# \*\*The Proposal: AI Human Impact Programme\*\***

The analysis above establishes both the need and the shape of what must be built. The four levels of the iceberg cannot be addressed by any single existing team. What is needed is a dedicated programme that spans measurement, experimentation, design, and governance — bridging the gap between "do no harm" and "actively do good."

We propose a strategic collaboration led by GovTech Innovation Office (GIO), the Responsible AI team (GovTech AI Practice) and the AI Policy team (NAIG), with external research institutions such as MIT Media Lab and Singapore-based social scientists, and 3–5 GovTech product development teams currently working on AI solutions with consequential human impact. We will create an interim programme structure to house this initiative for 12 months which if successful, we can formalise as a team within AI Practice and a new capability for GovTech.

A key aspect of the programme is live experimentation — working with GovTech product development teams to apply human impact practices to rapidly test, learn and iterate. Only by building and evaluating with real user behaviour can robust practices, capabilities and design patterns emerge.

The evidence base for this programme draws on a week-long immersion with the MIT Media Lab's Advancing Humans with AI (AHA) research group (April 2026), documented in the accompanying trip report *\*"AI for Human Flourishing — MIT Media Lab Trip Report"\**. The research projects, frameworks, and empirical findings referenced throughout this proposal are grounded in that collaboration.

The programme has four workstreams, each mapping to a level of the iceberg:

**\#\#\# \*\*Workstream 1: Singapore Human-AI Impact Framework\*\*** *\*(Level 1–2)\**

Develop a practical framework for measuring the human effects of AI systems in the Singapore context. This builds on MIT Media Lab's Open Benchmarks for Human Impact and AI Nutrition Labels — localised and adapted to Singapore's public sector, social norms, and domain-specific requirements.

The framework will extend the work of the Responsible AI and AI Policy teams to define the measurable human impact dimensions we hold ourselves accountable to:

\* Agency — does the user retain ownership?  
\* Capability retention — does repeated use strengthen or weaken independent ability?  
\* Judgement — can the user challenge and improve AI output?  
\* Plurality of options — does the system expand or narrow choices?  
\* Over-reliance and dependency — is the user becoming passive?  
\* Social connection — does AI support or displace human relationships?  
\* Meaningful work — does AI increase officers' sense of purpose and mastery?  
\* Trust and accountability — can users understand and contest AI-supported actions?

**\#\#\# \*\*Workstream 2: Live Experiments\*\*** *\*(Levels 2–3)\**

Apply the framework to selected priority AI deployments where human impact outcomes are consequential and upstream design and testing is needed. Initial domains:

1\. AI in education — AI learning companions or teacher support tools  
2\. AI for internal public-sector workflows — systems that automate analysis, casework, drafting or decision support for officers  
3\. AI career guidance — tools that shape consequential life decisions about work, reskilling and transitions  
4\. AI companionship — systems for vulnerable or socially isolated populations

Each experiment will assess both operating outcomes (speed, throughput, quality) and human outcomes (agency, capability, judgement, dependency).

**\#\#\# \*\*Workstream 3: Design Patterns for Human Flourishing\*\*** *\*(Level 2)\**

Build a living library of empirically-validated design principles and execution examples. The aim is to give product teams concrete, testable patterns — not abstract principles — so that designing for flourishing becomes as actionable as designing for usability.

Core design orientations drawn from MIT AHA research:

\* **\*\*Plurality\*\*** — multiple futures honestly simulated, with functionality to interact with them, so there is never a single answer or a reductive version of the future  
\* **\*\*Transparency\*\*** — the system makes visible what matters to the user's agency and growth, so they can see not just what the AI produces but what they are contributing and how that is impacting them  
\* **\*\*Accountability\*\*** — built in at the interaction layer, just-in-time, designed to strengthen user judgement rather than substitute for it

Tangible pattern examples: multiple futures rather than single recommendations; reflective prompts before action; confidence and uncertainty displays; "why this suggestion?" explanations; user intent confirmation before agent execution; friction for high-stakes decisions; escalation to human support in moments of vulnerability; post-action reflection; dependency warnings where repeated use suggests over-reliance.

**\#\#\# \*\*Workstream 4: Governance, Standards and Procurement Integration\*\*** *\*(Levels 3–4)\**

Translate findings into operational levers that make human impact a standing requirement rather than a one-off assessment. This workstream ensures the programme produces institutional change, not just research outputs.

Outputs will include:

\* A Human Impact Statement template (required for significant AI deployments)  
\* Evaluation guidance for AI product teams  
\* Procurement clauses for vendor-provided AI systems  
\* Post-launch review requirements and triggers  
\* Guidance for when human impact evaluation is mandatory  
\* Recommendations for integration into WOG AI governance

**\#\# \*\*Solution Hypothesis: The Human Impact Harness\*\***

*\*\[To be validated through the programme\]\**

In technical AI deployment, the solution to non-deterministic models is to build a harness: verification layers, audit logs, confidence thresholds, human escalation triggers and fallback mechanisms such that we can fine-tune agentic behaviours.

We need the same discipline for human impact.

A **\*\*Human Impact Harness\*\*** is the set of design, measurement and governance mechanisms that ensures an AI system strengthens rather than substitutes for human capability over time.

| Technology Harness | Human Impact Harness |  
| :--- | :--- |  
| Output verification | Capability and judgement evaluation |  
| Audit logs | Visibility into user reliance and decision paths |  
| Confidence thresholds | Thresholds for when AI should slow down, ask, explain or escalate |  
| Human escalation | Hand-off to teachers, mentors, officers, counsellors or caregivers |  
| Error detection | Detection of over-reliance, deskilling, dependency or narrowing of options |  
| Regression testing | Longitudinal testing of whether users retain capability over time |  
| System monitoring | Ongoing monitoring of human impact drift after deployment |

**\#\#\# \*\*Three Layers of the Harness\*\***

The harness operates through three distinct layers, each buildable as a standalone component:

| Layer | What it does | Implementation |  
| :--- | :--- | :--- |  
| **\*\*Instrumentation\*\*** | Observes user behaviour to detect signals of over-reliance, deskilling, or narrowing | Lightweight telemetry layer — tracks patterns like acceptance-without-edit rate, declining unaided task completion, reduced exploration of alternatives |  
| **\*\*Intervention\*\*** | Acts when thresholds are crossed — slows the system, surfaces reflection, or escalates to a human | Configurable rules engine: e.g. after consecutive accepts-without-edit, surface a reflection prompt; after declining independent performance, recommend mentor check-in |  
| **\*\*Evaluation\*\*** | Measures longitudinal impact on human capabilities through structured experiments | A/B test harness \+ before/after assessment protocol integrated into pilot deployments |

**\#\#\# \*\*Working Prototypes\*\***

To validate the harness concept, we propose building three reusable platform components within the next 3–6 months. These are designed as generic middleware — deployable on any government AI system, not tied to a single product. Each can be tested on whichever internal AI tool offers the clearest signal.

**\*\*Prototype A — "Capability Mirror" dashboard\*\***

A pluggable telemetry and visualisation layer that shows any AI system's users how their interaction patterns are trending: ratio of AI suggestions accepted vs modified, independent task performance over time, how often they explore multiple options. The dashboard is reflective, not punitive — it makes visible what is otherwise invisible. It attaches to any AI system with user interaction logs.

*\*Design principle:\** If users can see how their relationship with AI is evolving, many will self-correct without external mandates. The same logic that makes fitness trackers effective applies here: awareness precedes agency.

**\*\*Prototype B — "Productive Friction" injection layer\*\***

A configurable middleware component that sits between any AI system and its users to insert interaction patterns validated by research: hints before answers, multiple options before recommendations, "what would you do first?" prompts, reflection before action. Config-driven — product teams specify which friction patterns apply to their domain and context.

*\*Design principle:\** Not all friction is bad. MIT AHA's research on learning companions demonstrates that children whose AI preserved productive struggle showed greater learning ability, retention, and intrinsic motivation. The same applies to adults. The goal is to remove *\*wasteful\** friction while preserving *\*formative\** friction.

**\*\*Prototype C — "Impact Nutrition Label" generator\*\***

An automated assessment tool that analyses any AI system's interaction patterns against the Human Impact framework dimensions (agency, capability, judgement, plurality, dependency, social connection) and generates a standardised disclosure label. Inspired by MIT's AI Nutrition Labels. Can be run against any government AI deployment as part of review or procurement — giving product teams, leadership, and users a legible summary of how a system is likely to affect the people who use it.

*\*Design principle:\** Just as nutritional labels surface health impacts that consumers cannot directly observe, AI Impact Labels surface effects on human capabilities that users cannot see in the moment of interaction.

**\#\#\# \*\*Validation Experiments\*\***

The prototypes are hypotheses. We propose a structured experiment plan to validate them:

**\*\*Experiment 1 — Baseline measurement\*\***

Attach the Capability Mirror instrumentation layer to a selected internal AI tool. Measure current user acceptance rates, independent task performance, and self-reported agency. No interventions — pure observation to establish baseline and validate that the telemetry captures meaningful signals.

**\*\*Experiment 2 — A/B test productive friction approaches\*\***

Test different productive friction middleware approaches compared to a control group (e.g. "Here are 3 possible approaches — which would you start with?" before presenting a draft). Measure: independent task quality, decision confidence, time-to-completion. Critical constraint: productive friction must not degrade time-to-completion by more than 10%.

**\*\*Experiment 3 — Capability Mirror pilot\*\***

Give a subset of users access to the reflective dashboard. Measure: do users who see their patterns self-correct? Does awareness of over-reliance change behaviour without external mandates? Is the effect sustained or temporary?

**\*\*Experiment 4 — Longitudinal harness validation\*\***

For the pilot groups, measure capability retention after 3 months: can users perform equivalent tasks without over-dependency on AI? Has judgement quality improved or declined? Does the instrumentation reliably predict which users will show capability decline?

**\#\#\# \*\*Success Criteria\*\***

The harness concept is validated if:

\* Users in the "productive friction" group show ≥15% higher independent task performance after 3 months, without \>10% increase in time-to-completion  
\* At least XX% of users on the Capability Mirror report increased awareness of their AI interaction patterns  
\* The instrumentation layer can reliably detect known over-reliance signals (validated against self-report and supervisor assessment)  
\* Impact Nutrition Labels are independently judged as "useful" or "very useful" by \>XX% of product teams assessed

**\#\# \*\*What We Are NOT Proposing\*\***

To be explicit about scope and intent:

\* **\*\*Not a new governance gate.\*\*** This programme does not add approval steps to the AI deployment pipeline. It builds measurement and design capabilities that make faster deployment safer.  
\* **\*\*Not a brake on AI adoption.\*\*** The goal is to make rapid adoption more valuable — not slower. Speed without human impact measurement creates hidden institutional debt. Speed with measurement compounds.  
\* **\*\*Not a standalone academic research programme.\*\*** Every workstream is anchored to live government AI deployments. Research collaborations exist to generate evidence that directly informs design and policy decisions — not publications.  
\* **\*\*Not a replacement for existing Responsible AI work.\*\*** This programme extends and builds upon the foundation that the Responsible AI team has established. Level 1 continues. This adds Levels 2–4.

**\---**

**\#\# \*\*What Success Looks Like at 12+ Months\*\***

By end 2027, we aspire for the programme to deliver:

1\. **\*\*Singapore has a working Human Impact Framework\*\*** — with validated metrics, tested on real government AI systems, that can tell us whether a deployment is strengthening or weakening human capability. No other country has this at the government level.

2\. **\*\*Product teams have a practical toolkit\*\*** — the Human Impact Harness components (Capability Mirror, productive friction layer, Impact Nutrition Labels) are available as reusable infrastructure that any AI team can deploy alongside their systems.

3\. **\*\*3–5 live experiments have produced evidence\*\*** — we know, from real Singapore data, what specific design patterns preserve agency and build capability in education, public officer workflows, career guidance, and social support contexts.

4\. **\*\*Procurement and governance include human impact\*\*** — AI systems procured or developed for government use require a Human Impact Statement. Post-launch reviews include human impact measures. The standard has shifted.

5\. **\*\*Singapore's position is clear\*\*** — we have an explicit, evidence-backed stance on what AI should do to people, not just what it should do for them. This positions Singapore as a global reference for responsible AI that goes beyond harm-avoidance.

**\---**

**\#\# \*\*Summary\*\***

The gap between "AI that avoids harm" and "AI that actively strengthens people" is where the most consequential design decisions in government AI now sit. We have the floor — responsible AI frameworks that prevent direct harm. What we lack is the ceiling: systematic measurement, evidence-based design, and operational standards that ensure the AI systems we deploy make the people using them more capable over time.

The Human Impact Harness is our solution hypothesis to this gap — a set of reusable design, measurement and governance mechanisms that can be applied to any government AI deployment to ensure it strengthens rather than substitutes for human capability. The programme will build this harness through live experimentation, not theory.

In 12+ months, the programme will deliver: a validated Human Impact Framework for Singapore; reusable harness components (instrumentation, intervention, evaluation) deployable on any government AI system; empirical evidence from 3–5 live experiments on what design patterns preserve human agency; and integration into procurement, governance and product development processes.

The timing is critical. AI is already shaping how Singapore's public service thinks, decides, and works — at a scale no other government has matched. We currently have no systematic way to know whether that shaping is building capability or eroding it. Every month without measurement is a month of accumulating invisible institutional debt.

**\*\*The decision sought\*\*** from the Innovation Panel, supported by MDDI and GovTech leadership, is to designate the AI for Human Flourishing / Human-AI Impact programme as a priority strategic bet that the Innovation Office will drive over the next 12 months, with in-principle approval for Innovation Funding to support research collaborations with external institutions and prototype development.

The institutions that move fast on AI with the right harnesses in place will compound in human capability. Those that move fast without them will accumulate human debt — weaker judgement, reduced ownership, increased dependency — that becomes harder and more expensive to reverse with every passing quarter. AI systems that strengthen human agency, judgement, and capability will help Singaporeans compound. Those that quietly substitute for these capabilities will deepen the K-shaped divide. This programme ensures we are building toward the former.

**\#\# \*\*Annex A: Public Officers as Users of AI\*\***

As the government rapidly diffuses AI tools internally, officers will increasingly work with AI agents that support the drafting of reports, data analysis, strategic thinking, triaging, decision making and implementing actions. These systems will shape how officers think, decide and understand their own role.

The human impact goal should not be for AI to make public officers more productive and produce more work, faster and cheaper. Rather the goal should be to move officers up the stack: from routine execution toward increasing judgement, integration, empathy, supervision and accountability.

A well-designed internal AI system should help officers:

\* Understand the problem more clearly  
\* See relevant evidence and trade-offs  
\* Challenge assumptions  
\* Compare options  
\* Exercise judgement  
\* Maintain accountability  
\* Learn from repeated use

A poorly designed system may do the opposite. It may produce polished outputs that officers approve without understanding. It may reduce opportunities to practise judgement. It may increase throughput while weakening institutional memory and professional capability.

For this reason, internal government AI deployments should be part of the first phase of human impact evaluation.

**\---**

**\#\# \*\*Annex B: Populations at Risk\*\***

The populations most at risk are not defined by demographics alone. They are defined by the conditions under which AI is most likely to substitute for agency, judgement, social connection or capability rather than strengthen it — *\*where capability is still forming, where social connection is fragile, where cognitive stress is already high.\**

Priority groups include:

**\*\*Children and students\*\***  
Because their reasoning habits, motivation and self-confidence are still forming. AI can support learning, but it can also shortcut the struggle through which real capability develops.

**\*\*Mid-career workers\*\***  
Because AI career and reskilling tools may shape consequential decisions about work, identity, income and future pathways. Poorly designed systems may create false certainty or narrow options too early.

**\*\*Public officers in routine or rules-heavy roles\*\***  
Because agentic AI may automate parts of their work before jobs are redesigned around higher-value judgement, supervision and accountability.

**\*\*Seniors and socially isolated individuals\*\***  
Because AI companions and support tools may either strengthen real-world connection or deepen dependency and withdrawal.

**\*\*Citizens navigating high-stakes public services\*\***  
Because users seeking help with employment, housing, health, family or financial support may be under stress and more likely to defer to confident AI recommendations.

The purpose of the programme is not to slow AI adoption for these groups. It is to ensure that AI adoption improves their agency and life chances rather than quietly compounding disadvantage.

**\#\# \*\*Annex C: Example Human Impact Metrics\*\***

The programme will test a first set of human impact metrics across the pilot systems.

| Dimension | What we are testing | Example indicators |  
| :--- | :--- | :--- |  
| Agency | Does the user retain ownership of the decision or action? | User can explain final choice; user modifies AI recommendation; user reports ownership |  
| Capability | Does repeated use strengthen or weaken independent ability? | Performance without AI; transfer to similar tasks; retention over time |  
| Judgement | Can the user challenge, calibrate and improve AI output? | Error detection; quality of edits; ability to identify assumptions |  
| Plurality | Does the system expand or narrow options? | Number and diversity of options explored; user perception of possibility |  
| Productive friction | Does the system preserve useful effort? | Attempts before answer; reflection before action; avoided premature closure |  
| Dependency | Is the user becoming over-reliant? | Acceptance without review; declining unaided performance; anxiety without AI |  
| Social connection | Does AI support or displace human relationships? | Referrals to humans; maintained human contact; reduced isolation |  
| Meaningful work | Does AI increase or reduce officers' sense of purpose and mastery? | Officer confidence; time on judgement; perceived value of work |  
| Trust and accountability | Can users understand and contest AI-supported actions? | Explainability, appeal, audit trail, confidence calibration |

These metrics are illustrative and will be tested, iterated and validated through the programme. The goal is metrics that are simple to understand, relatable, actionable and meaningful in the Singapore, public sector and domain-specific contexts.

**\#\# \*\*Annex D: Human Impact Statement v0.1\*\***

Every significant government AI system should include a Human Impact Statement before deployment. The statement answers:

**\*\*1. Intended human benefits\*\***  
What human capability, agency or wellbeing outcome is this AI system intended to strengthen?

**\*\*2. Capabilities at risk\*\***  
What human capability might weaken through repeated use of this system?

**\*\*3. Productive friction\*\***  
Where should the system deliberately preserve friction because it helps users think, learn, choose or retain ownership?

**\*\*4. Plurality\*\***  
Where should the system show multiple options, futures or interpretations rather than one answer?

**\*\*5. Transparency and user understanding\*\***  
How will the user understand what the AI is doing, what it believes the user intends, what it plans to do next, and what assumptions it is making?

**\*\*6. Potential for over-reliance and dependency\*\***  
What signals will be monitored to detect over-reliance, deskilling or unhealthy dependency?

**\*\*7. Human escalation\*\***  
When should the system hand off to a human?

**\*\*8. Evidence of human strengthening\*\***  
What evidence will show that users are better off after repeated use?

**\#\# \*\*Annex E: Design Principles for Human Flourishing\*\***

The programme will test and refine design principles for AI systems that support human flourishing:

1\. **\*\*Preserve agency\*\*** — AI should help users make better decisions, not quietly make decisions for them. Systems should make clear what the user owns, what the AI is suggesting, and what remains uncertain.

2\. **\*\*Strengthen capability over time\*\*** — Repeated use should make users more capable, not less. Where learning, judgement or expertise matters, systems should avoid giving the final answer too quickly.

3\. **\*\*Preserve productive struggle\*\*** — Remove wasteful friction but preserve formative friction. Some friction helps users think, practise, reflect and own the outcome.

4\. **\*\*Show plurality\*\*** — For consequential decisions, AI should help users explore multiple plausible paths, trade-offs and consequences rather than presenting one recommendation as the answer.

5\. **\*\*Make the work visible\*\*** — Users should see what the AI believes they intend, what it is doing, what assumptions it is making, and what it plans to do next. Especially important for agents that act on behalf of users.

6\. **\*\*Calibrate trust\*\*** — AI systems should help users know when to trust, question or escalate. Confidence, uncertainty, evidence and limitations should be visible.

7\. **\*\*Detect over-reliance\*\*** — AI systems should monitor patterns suggesting users are becoming passive, dependent or less capable, and intervene when it appears to be substituting for human judgement.

8\. **\*\*Escalate with care\*\*** — In moments of vulnerability, ambiguity or high stakes, AI should know when to slow down, defer, or hand off to a human.

9\. **\*\*Support meaningful work\*\*** — For public officers, AI should increase time spent on judgement, empathy, sense-making and accountability — not reduce officers to passive approvers of machine output.

10\. **\*\*Evaluate longitudinally\*\*** — Human impact often appears over time. AI systems should be evaluated not only for immediate task success but for repeated-use effects on capability and agency.

**\#\# \*\*Annex F: Live Experiment Designs\*\***

The first phase focuses on use cases where AI is likely to shape human capability, judgement or agency over time. Specific pilot systems, agencies and product owners will be confirmed with MDDI, GovTech and agency partners.

**\---**

**\#\#\#** *\*Pilot 1: AI Learning Companions\**

**\*\*Domain:\*\*** Education, tutoring, learning companions, student study support, or teacher-supported AI learning tools.

**\*\*Why this matters:\*\*** Education is one of the highest-impact domains because students are still forming reasoning habits, confidence and learning behaviours. AI tutors can help students learn faster, but they can also shortcut the productive struggle needed to develop independent capability.

**\*\*Core evaluation question:\*\*** Does the AI system strengthen independent reasoning and motivation, or does it promote answer-seeking and dependency behaviours?

**\*\*Human impact measures:\*\*** Student ability to solve similar problems without AI; retention over time; transfer to novel problems; confidence in own reasoning; frequency of answer-seeking vs attempt-making; teacher perception of student independence; student motivation and sense of ownership.

**\*\*Design patterns to test:\*\*** Hints before answers; Socratic questioning; "try first" prompts; explanations that require student participation; reflection after solution; teacher visibility into AI-supported learning patterns.

**\---**

**\#\#\#** *\*Pilot 2: AI Career Guidance / Coaching Agent\**

**\*\*Domain:\*\*** Career guidance, reskilling, job matching, workforce transition, lifelong learning or adult training pathways.

**\*\*Why this matters:\*\*** Career guidance is directly linked to the K-shaped economy. AI can help Singaporeans navigate changing labour markets, but it can also prematurely narrow options or create false certainty. The highest-value use is not to give people a single answer quickly, but to help them explore plausible futures, understand trade-offs and make decisions they can own.

**\*\*Core evaluation question:\*\*** Does the AI career agent expand users' sense of possibility, ownership and motivation, or does it lead users to outsource consequential life decisions?

**\*\*Human impact measures:\*\*** Number and diversity of pathways explored; user understanding of trade-offs; confidence without over-certainty; ability to explain final decision; regret or satisfaction after time has passed; perceived ownership of decision; whether users challenge or simply accept AI recommendations.

**\*\*Design patterns to test:\*\*** Multiple future pathways; simulated future selves (per MIT AHA "Future You" research); trade-off exploration; "what would make this wrong?" prompts; bias and assumption surfacing; human mentor handoff for high-stakes decisions.

**\---**

**\#\#\#** *\*Pilot 3: AI for Public Officer Workflows\**

**\*\*Domain:\*\*** Internal government AI tools for drafting, summarisation, data analysis, casework, triaging, decision support, service operations or policy support.

**\*\*Why this matters:\*\*** Public officers are users of government AI. Internal AI tools will shape their judgement, confidence, craft and sense of meaningful work. If poorly designed, these tools increase throughput while deskilling officers. If well designed, they help officers move up the stack: from routine execution to supervision, integration, judgement and accountability.

**\*\*Core evaluation question:\*\*** Do human-AI workflows enable public officers to improve their judgement and decision-making, or promote outsourcing of thinking and passive approval of machine output?

**\*\*Human impact measures:\*\*** Officer ability to explain, challenge and improve AI outputs; quality of final decisions; error detection by officers; time spent on judgement vs routine production; officer confidence and perceived meaningful work; retention of domain knowledge over time.

**\*\*Design patterns to test:\*\*** AI-generated draft plus critique prompts; explicit uncertainty and assumptions; "review before action" checkpoints; comparison of multiple options; decision trail capture; escalation triggers for ambiguity; officer learning loops after final decision.

**\---**

**\#\#\#** *\*Pilot 4: AI Companionship / Social Support\**

**\*\*Domain:\*\*** AI companionship, mental wellbeing, ageing, caregiver support, loneliness, community referral or social service navigation.

**\*\*Why this matters:\*\*** AI systems designed for emotional support or companionship are especially consequential for users in moments of vulnerability. These systems can either strengthen real-world support and social connection, or deepen dependency and withdrawal.

**\*\*Core evaluation question:\*\*** Does the AI system support resilience, real-world connection and human relationship building, or does it create emotional dependency and withdrawal?

**\*\*Human impact measures:\*\*** Changes in perceived loneliness; referrals to human support; maintenance or increase of real-world social contact; user ability to distinguish AI support from human relationship; dependency signals over repeated use; escalation in moments of distress.

**\*\*Design patterns to test:\*\*** Warm handoff to human support; limits on emotional intensification; encouragement of real-world contact; distress detection and escalation; clear disclosure of AI role; reflection prompts that build resilience.
